# On Walking

There is a particular kind of thinking that only happens at three miles an hour. It is slower than the mind wants to go and faster than it can stand still, and somewhere in that mismatch the mind gives up trying to steer.

## Why we walk

Most walks have no destination worth mentioning. The corner shop, the post box, the long way round to a friend's house. The errand is an excuse, and everyone involved knows it.

### The body sets the pace

When you walk, the body decides how fast the day moves. You cannot skim a street the way you skim a page. Every doorway arrives in its own time.

### The mind wanders on purpose

A walk gives attention somewhere to rest that isn't a screen. The eyes take in hedges, roofs, a cat on a wall, and the thoughts you were circling finally land.

## Ways of walking

### The errand

Short, practical, and quietly restorative. Leave the headphones at home once in a while.

### The wander

No route, no time limit. Turn left when it looks interesting. The only rule is that you don't check the map until you're properly lost.

### The long walk

A whole morning, a packed lunch, and a hill at the end of it. You come home tired in the way that makes sleep easy.

## What to bring

Very little:

- Shoes you've already broken in
- Something warm, even if it looks fine outside
- A notebook small enough to forget you're carrying

## Walking and writing

Many writers have walked to think. The sentence that wouldn't come at the desk turns up somewhere around the second mile, fully formed, and has to be repeated under the breath all the way home.

> I can only meditate when I am walking. When I stop, I cease to think; my mind only works with my legs.

### Keeping what you find

Write it down as soon as you're home, before the kettle boils. Ideas found on foot are shy and leave quickly.

## Coming home

The best part of a walk is often the last hundred yards: the familiar gate, the key in the door, the room exactly as you left it but somehow larger.
